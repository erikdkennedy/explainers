We begin with an atom in its ground state.

The spinning arrow isn’t a property of the atom; it’s the special quantum probability of finding the atom in this state. Note: the classical probability is constant (it’s based on the length of the arrow). But the spinning means that, were the atom to split into phantom copies that end up in the same state, they’d have a chance to cancel each other out.

Guess what the next step is… 😉

A precise microwave blast splits the atom into two phantom copies: one ground-state and one excited. The power of the blast is calibrated so that, if you were to look immediately, there’s a 50% chance of finding the atom in its ground state, and 50% chance of finding it in its excited state. Hence their arrow lengths are equal.

Each phantom copy’s probability rotates at a speed proportional to the atom’s energy – meaning the excited atom’s arrow spins faster than the ground atom’s arrow. Now we simply wait until the two arrows are pointed in precisely opposite directions.

At precisely the moment when the copies’ quantum probabilities are equal and opposite, we shoot the second pulse of microwave photons. That splits each phantom copy into two more copies.

There is still only one atom; we are simply splitting possible states we could find it in.

Note that the ground copy formed from the energized copy has its direction flipped.

Next…

  1. The equal and opposite probabilities of the excited copies cancel each other out
  2. The in-sync probabilities of the ground copies add together

We are left, 100% of the time we look at the energy state of the atom, with only a ground state copy. All of this despite having shot two “randomizing” bursts of photons at it! A magic coin.
